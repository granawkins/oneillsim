# Stanford Torus — project research notes

## Source of truth and scope

Primary reference: **NASA SP-413, *Space Settlements: A Design Study***, based on the NASA–ASEE Engineering Systems Design Summer Program held June–August 1975 and published by NASA in 1977. The source PDF is not committed or stored under the app's public root; on the production host it is kept at `~/.local/share/oneillsim/study-source/nasa-sp-413-space-settlements-a-design-study.pdf`. For a local rebuild, obtain the source from NASA NTRS and store it outside the served project directory, then set `STUDY_SOURCE_PDF`. Treat the paper as the controlling historical design brief for the simulation; keep later implementation choices and interpretations clearly distinguished from what the paper actually specifies. The study is a conceptual engineering design, not a current validated habitat specification.

The study evaluates a 10,000-person settlement and recommends a toroidal habitat as a balanced baseline. It frames success broadly: safe physiology, air/water/food, pseudogravity, radiation protection, practical construction and maintenance, and psychological, social, cultural, architectural, and aesthetic needs. The habitat is a community, not merely a rotating shell.

## Baseline torus and gravity

- The baseline torus has major radius about **830 m** and minor/cross-section radius about **65 m**. That gives a 1,660 m centerline diameter, 130 m tube diameter, and nominal torus extents of 765–895 m from the spin axis (1,530–1,790 m diameters). The study favors the torus for a balance of mass, habitability, openness, architectural flexibility, and circulation.
- Target population: **10,000**. The study estimates **670,000 m² projected living/community area** for its baseline accounting; land-use allocations include housing, commerce, schools, hospital, assembly, recreation, public open space, services, storage, transportation, and mechanical systems. Do not equate every area metric in the paper with floor area or usable surface without checking its definition.
- Rotation is limited to approximately **1 rpm or less**; baseline gravity is about **0.95 g** (the criteria table gives 0.95 ± 0.05 g). At 1 rpm, the centerline speed at R=830 m is 87 m/s and the modeled outward rim at 895 m moves at 94 m/s. Artificial gravity comes from rotation; local “down” points radially outward from the spin axis. The ground sheet is currently modeled at the tube centerline for visual alignment; that is a project presentation choice, not a floor radius stated by SP-413.
- The paper's geometry is not interchangeable with the prototype's original 650 m major radius. The code uses the SP-413 830 m major radius with the 65 m tube radius; for the current visual model, the ground sheet is placed at the tube centerline (R=830 m) so it appears centered in the curved body. Treat that ground placement as a project presentation convention, not a floor radius specified by the paper. The existing 408-column texture grid is retained to preserve saved-world layout, so columns are angular sectors rather than exact 10 m square tiles.

## Physical systems and spatial organization

1. **Rotating pressure habitat / shell.** A large toroidal pressure vessel contains inhabited neighborhoods and internal structures. Structural design, pressure loads, rotational loads, compartmentation, leak detection, repair, and construction sequence matter. The study considers modular interior construction and incremental assembly.
2. **Living surface and community.** The inside of the torus is laid out as a human-scale settlement, with residences, workplaces, public and semi-public facilities, circulation, open space, and services. Design variety and social/psychological well-being are explicit goals; avoid representing it as one undifferentiated corridor.
3. **Agriculture and food production.** Controlled agriculture is integrated into the settlement's life-support and economic system. The study compares land allocation and crop/productivity assumptions and considers agriculture placement as part of habitat geometry. Model farms, crops, food flows, wastes, and labor as linked systems rather than decorative props. The paper does not provide a fully specified modern closed-loop ecology.
4. **Solar illumination.** Large mirrors/reflectors direct sunlight into the habitat; optical geometry and shielding/illumination tradeoffs shape the exterior design. Light cycles and shadowing should follow explicit simulation assumptions.
5. **Radiation and meteoroid protection.** Protection is a major mass and design driver. The paper evaluates passive mass shielding and magnetic concepts, secondary-particle effects, solar-flare sheltering, and meteoroid impacts; shielding may need to be structurally separate from the rapidly rotating habitat. Do not reduce this to a thin decorative outer ring.
6. **Thermal control and radiators.** Waste heat from people, agriculture, industry, and power systems must be rejected. Radiators are large engineered components with orientation and operational constraints, not generic exterior panels.
7. **Power and industrial production.** The settlement is tied to large-scale space industry and solar power satellite production, with material processing and manufacturing supplied substantially from lunar resources. The study's industrial/economic model is integral to its rationale; distinguish settlement life-support systems from export-production facilities.
8. **Transportation, hub, and logistics.** The settlement sits near the Earth–Moon Lagrange point L5 in the study's baseline, with transportation links to Earth, Moon, and industrial facilities. The rotating torus, nonrotating or slowly rotating infrastructure, docking/transfer, and cargo flows require deliberate interfaces; do not assume the torus itself is the complete transportation system.
9. **Life support and human environment.** Air composition/pressure, water, food, waste handling, temperature, health care, and maintenance support a resident population. The criteria include 23 ± 8 °C, oxygen partial pressure 22.7 ± 9 kPa, magnetic field ≤100 μT, and radiation exposure ≤0.5 rem/year for the general population (historical study criteria; not current medical guidance). The study stresses uncertainty and adopts conservative Earth-like conditions.

## What the paper does and does not establish

The report includes concept-level calculations, alternatives, illustrations, subsystem discussions, and assumptions. It is a strong source for the proposed baseline and design rationale, but not a turnkey engineering specification: many subsystems are preliminary, some values are assumptions, and the report explicitly calls for further study. “Real-time recycling of water and nutrients,” detailed atmosphere chemistry/control, complete ecological closure, multiplayer interaction, and walkable museum/web UX are project goals, not claims that SP-413 fully designed those systems. Research them separately and identify them as later extensions.

The study's social and psychological sections call for diversity, variability, privacy, public spaces, cultural accommodation, and recognizable terrestrial cues. A museum-like interactive experience should communicate both the physical engineering and the lived community, while allowing visitors to understand the tradeoffs and uncertainty behind the design.

## Simulation implications and fidelity checklist

- Keep a source ledger for every important numeric parameter: report value and page/figure/table, prototype value, units, and any conversion or interpretation.
- Make the habitat's rotation, gravity vector, day/light cycle, interior geometry, and exterior systems internally coherent. Clearly state when a simplified model substitutes for physical simulation.
- Represent neighborhoods, farms, circulation, public space, life-support infrastructure, radiation shielding, mirrors, radiators, hubs, and logistics as connected systems with inspectable explanations.
- Treat water/nutrient cycling as explicit stocks and flows (inputs, recovery, losses, storage, maintenance, and failure modes); do not imply perfect closure unless modeled and evidenced.
- Separate historically documented baseline content from the present prototype and future roadmap features. Preserve the current angular world-grid layout when changing geometry, and label prototype simplifications.
- Build for an explorable public museum: visitors can navigate, inspect objects and places, understand scale, and eventually meet one another at shared locations. Multiplayer/social features are a product direction, not part of the paper.
